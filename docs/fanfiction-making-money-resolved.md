# Fanfiction Making Money: Resolved?

Oct 4, 2026 · Jonathan

## Introduction

"Resolved" only applies to how things are now. Our environment can always change.

Others have likely resolved this same thing, I just didn't find or run across their material explaining it.

However, I'm fairly certain that as things stand now, we can put to rest the issue of whether or not fan-created works will ever have a path to commercialization.

Before I begin, I'd like to draw a distinction between "fanworks" and "subcreation". Fanworks is the term Archive of Our Own and the wider fan community use for every form of expression by fans, whether written fiction, art or anything else. Fanworks, by their very nature, are created by people in a community with few boundaries to their creativity and most often without express permission from Intellectual Property (IP) owners. As such, the very nature of fanworks is incompatible with the legal, marketing, branding and financial realities and boundaries that are introduced when cold hard cash comes into the picture.

What I'll be talking about here is subcreation. Subcreation is authorized by the IP owner and thus creates a path to commercialization. The question we're exploring here is whether or not the IP owner can sustainably allow subcreators to participate in their IP while also commercially benefiting.

*Note: "RPG" means Role-Playing Game and refers to Table-Top Role-Playing Games, like Dungeons and Dragons.*

## The question I was trying to answer

Fans love worlds that belong to someone else, and they make things in them. I set out to find whether that love could become subcreation, something fans are openly allowed to do, with money moving fairly between the subcreator and the person who made the world.

Personally, I tried to design a platform called Open Eppic to enable the commercial reality of subcreators and IP owners building a world together. Original creators would open their worlds, subcreators would build on them under a real license, and the platform would protect both sides from being exploited, including by the platform itself.

My own creator guide named the doubt at the center of it: "Will readers pay for derivative fiction (vs. expecting free fanfic)?" This paper is the answer I arrived at.

## The short answer

The super-short answer is that the market naturally resolved this issue, arriving where it needed to be without consciously realizing it. That answer doesn't include something like Open Eppic, though there have been major attempts: Amazon's Kindle Worlds and, more recently with a blockchain twist, Story Protocol.

[Kindle Worlds closed in 2018](https://fanlore.org/wiki/Kindle_Worlds). In June 2026, Story Protocol [renamed itself the DATA Foundation](https://www.coindesk.com/business/2026/06/25/a16z-backed-crypto-firm-rebrands-shifts-focus-to-solving-ai-s-global-copyright-headache) and moved away from the broader IP market toward AI training data.

Four reasons explain why, and the findings below take each in turn:

1. **Ownership makes the lines sticky.** Copyright protects ownership, not free use, so once money is involved, no simple, affordable system can say what a borrowed piece of a world is worth.
2. **The licenses that work already exist.** Creative Commons gives rights away. Lancer's Third Party License and the ORC License trade value through the brand, royalty-free.
3. **Registration defeats the right.** Money requires records, records become registration, and registration gives the IP owner the tools to revoke.
4. **For most brands, it isn't worth it.** A brand is a promise of more of the same, and subcreation only pays where it sells the original, as it does in tabletop RPGs.

## What I found

### Finding 1: Ownership makes the lines sticky

Content *ownership*, through copyright, is the right the government backs and protects, not the free use of IP. Copyright belongs to the creator from the moment the work is fixed, and every arrangement for sharing it is the owner's choice. That makes subcreation sticky and legally complicated from the outset.

If a person creates content with pieces of it "owned" by another, where is the line drawn? What if I only mention Frodo Baggins in passing in my fantasy story? What if I introduce my own character, or minor details, in the Star Wars universe, barely adding anything new?

Now introduce money into the mix. How much is that mention of Frodo Baggins worth? What is owed to the IP owner, and what can the subcreator keep?

When things get this sticky and complicated, it becomes near-impossible to set up simple and affordable systems to handle them.

### Finding 2: The licenses already exist

An IP owner who wants to give anyone the right of subcreation, commercial rights included, already can, with the various Creative Commons licenses. An IP owner who wants value to come back to them already can too. They can craft a license like Lancer's [Third Party License](https://massifpress.com/legal), or adopt the pre-made [Open RPG Creative (ORC) License](https://azoralaw.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/ORC-License.FINAL_.pdf).

In both cases, the brand stays attached as the way value is given to the subcreator and received back by the IP owner. Lancer's license opens its "locations, concepts, characters, events, factions, and equipment," and every product must state "Lancer is copyright Massif Press."

Note that both licenses are *royalty-free*. The "payment" in both directions is *intangible* value. The subcreator gets some access to the brand's audience and the potential for some monetary profit, and the IP owner gets more brand exposure, and hopefully more sales as people are directed back toward their products.

The IP owner's brand stays protected, too. Neither license lets a subcreator present their work as official, and Lancer's requires every product to say it "is not affiliated with Massif Press." If the subcreator fails, it doesn't reflect much on the IP owner. If they succeed, the IP owner's brand benefits.

It is this exchange of value, and mitigation against damage, that the market has naturally arrived at. Neither side needs a platform in the middle, and neither has to register with the other. The pieces Open Eppic was going to supply already exist, free, public, and in use.

Not all brands engage in this. Not every brand is hungry for exposure; some already have established audiences, and some want tighter control over how their brand is expanded and what first impression it makes on potential customers.

This kind of value exchange most likely works best where audiences are small and niche, and neither the customers nor the IP owner have much money to spend. There, formal marketing doesn't make financial sense, so the brand has to grow and spread in other ways. Offering your most engaged fans a way to make a little more money, and to create a wealth of content, is one viable way.

### Finding 3: Long attribution chains carry no value

ORC is a great license structure, with one requirement that becomes a burden as content builds on content. Every product must credit "all upstream licensors" of the material it is derived from, in the form each one asked for. Done by hand across a long chain, that can become an impossible task.

My first instinct was that Open Eppic could automate it. Each ORC product already prints its notice, so a tool could read the notices of everything you used and compose yours. Each IP owner's customizations live in that notice, so even those could be carried.

Of course, the deeper question is this: if attribution chains get too long, is value really being transferred anymore?

Look at what the chain credits. ORC reserves characters, settings, worlds and plots unless an owner expressly opens them, so an ORC chain mostly credits a lineage of game rules. The value reaches the buyer through one name on the cover: the single game the product is compatible with.

The chain is bookkeeping. The brand is the value. The chains that grow longest are the ones where each credit is worth the least to the brands being credited, and because a product with a long chain is by nature a mashup, the credits are worth much less to the subcreator too.

So automating them solves a clerical problem, not a value problem. Not even the attribution chain survives as a reason for Open Eppic to exist.

### Finding 4: Registration defeats the right

If money is involved, records are legally required, and records effectively become a form of registration. What was registered can be revoked. As such, registration doesn't work for rights a person should already have.

People already experience this on YouTube and other services, where automated systems take down or demonetize videos flagged as containing someone else's content when they don't.

Gun ownership is the clearest example. You don't have to agree with it; that's not the point. The U.S. treats gun ownership as a right, and attempts to require registration of every firearm nationally have not succeeded.

What is the purpose of registration, truly? People already have the right to own and bear firearms, so why do they need to register them? There are many hot takes on this, but it comes down to giving the government the ability to audit, investigate and revoke the right. The government is given power and authority it shouldn't have over the rights of law-abiding citizens.

The cost of a right this highly protected is that it can only be revoked after the citizen abuses it. Enforcement is forced to be reactionary, coming only after a crime has occurred. Arguments for gun registration often want to be proactive, preventing the crime in the first place.

The problem is the dangerous precedent that sets. Enforcement, and by extension judgment, can be exercised *before* any criminal act has occurred.

It is the same for subcreation. A register of subcreated works gives the IP owner the tools to abuse "license-abiding" subcreators who haven't breached the license's terms, through arbitrary revocations of their rights.

The right, then, must protect the subcreator from the more powerful party in the relationship: the IP owner. Registration tips the power balance back in the IP owner's favor, and few subcreators have the means to fight wrongful enforcement. The crux of the matter is that registration erodes the very freedoms and protections the license is supposed to provide.

### Finding 5: A brand is a promise of more of the same

Offering subcreation rights often isn't worth enough for a brand, which is why offers like Lancer's are rare and few. A brand is essentially a promise to customers of more of the same thing. If subcreations don't deliver more of the same thing, the brand starts to be "diluted."

When it works, the brand carries the value. Star Wars books written in the universe added value, and had the incentive to be made in the first place, because people wanted more Star Wars. The writer could sell more "Star Wars" even though it wasn't their IP.

But take the Doom video game, a strong brand. When a "Doom" product comes out, there are a host of expectations, from quality of execution and visuals to the kind of story being told and the game's "feel" when it's played. A subcreation that falls short on any one of these doesn't fully represent the brand.

Worse, if that subcreation becomes popular, it can alter expectations of the brand. What the brand is supposed to deliver becomes less focused and fractured, and so do its audience and customers, whose preferences can split in two. Marketing your own product is hard enough; creating internal competition and managing it adds another layer of difficulty.

Imagine, for a moment, if the company that owns Oreo cookies let smaller companies create their own Oreo flavors. For one, it doesn't make sense: Oreo can make its own flavor variations and keep all of the profits; doing it in-house is more profitable. Two, it could cause an explosion of Oreo flavors on the shelf and in media channels, as each small company tries to sell its version to Oreo customers.

With Oreo collecting only royalties from those small manufacturers, while competing with them to market its own "original" products, it has created a difficult space to operate in.

In some industries there's also the question of time. Grand Theft Auto VI, coming out soon, is a massive release because it's been over a decade since the last one. Gamers can't absorb frequent releases of tons of subcreated content: video games take time to play, and it's especially difficult when each release also costs money.

Star Wars also shows what happens to subcreations when the brand changes hands. On April 25, 2014, after Disney bought Lucasfilm, the Expanded Universe was [relabeled "Legends"](https://www.jedinews.com/film-music-tv/articles/official-site-the-legendary-star-wars-expanded-universe-turns-a-new-page/). The books stayed "in print, presented under the new Legends banner," but they lost the canon promise overnight.

The brand was the asset. Whoever owns it can revalue every subcreation at once.

### Finding 6: Promises don't stack

A brand's promise can't be kept if too many brands are added. You won't get more Halo if Master Chief is interacting with Stargate, Andromeda and a host of other sci-fi franchises. You'll get a mashup.

The reason for Open Eppic was very complex relationships between worlds, and those relationships are effectively diluted by the presence of many more IPs. For mashups to sell, a brand would have to be built whose sole purpose is high-quality mashups.

And really, it would likely be more like "Master Chief versus other worlds": one IP at the center, and how it interacts with others. The crossovers that sell already work this way. Super Smash Bros. and Kingdom Hearts each have one host and many guests under one brand, and each is a deal between rights holders, not a platform.

Even Kindle Worlds, Amazon's licensed fan fiction store, [prohibited crossovers](https://fanlore.org/wiki/Kindle_Worlds).

### Finding 7: Stories are experiments, and crossovers strain the experiment

There is story craft philosophy here. Most all stories are really an exploration of a "what if" hypothesis, and seeing where it goes. That is why stories can often "turn bad" at the ending: the author fiddles with the experiment, or is unable to "solve" the experiment of the hypothesis.

A crossover is an experiment with two sets of established laws, and only one set can govern. Centering on one IP means choosing which world's laws are the controlled conditions. When a writer bends both worlds to make the meeting work, power levels inflate, tones collide, and the ending fails for the same reason any ending fails.

So the deep, many-world collaboration I was designing for was working against good story craft. A good story needs a stable experiment, and a stable experiment needs one world in charge.

### Finding 8: The market already arrived

So the market, without consciously realizing it, has arrived where it needs to be. In Lancer's case, its industry, business model and license all reflect the realities above.

Creating more RPG content is *difficult* and time-consuming. Lancer's audience can absorb content faster than Massif Press can produce it, which keeps demand high for their future releases. They aren't competing for marketing; allowing others to create in their IP *is* their marketing.

The nature of RPGs means that *to use subcreated content, you need the original game*. Lancer's core rules are effectively required to use any subcreated content and to play at all. Massif even gives away a [free edition of the core book](https://massif-press.itch.io/corebook-pdf-free) with "all the core rules, mechs, and character options," keeping the GM section, NPC creation and setting for the paid one.

There is a dependency of subcreated content on the original. Oreo cookies don't work like this, and neither does most fiction. Works of subcreated fiction are often stand-alone and don't depend on a person reading the original works, though that may improve the experience. Art certainly doesn't require it.

So tabletop role-playing games may be one of the only industries where letting subcreators create in your world actually works. Even then, royalties from subcreator to IP owner don't make sense; that would require much bigger IPs. At that point, the subcreations are likely only merchandise like t-shirts or toys, not content in direct competition with the original works.

Where subcreation would compete with or dilute the core product, licensing doesn't pay. A subcreated novel competes with the author's next novel and blurs the canon. Some authors won't even read fanworks, for fear a fan's idea will look like their own next book.

Though some bemoan the "unsupported" state of fanfiction, the truth is that it's simply not supported by very many IP holders. The value of the licensing design isn't enough for most brands; it's likely only worth it for specific kinds, like Lancer RPG.

Everywhere else, fanworks stay what they have always been: a gift economy that many IP holders tolerate and few support. That isn't a failure waiting for a platform to fix it. It's the equilibrium.

That's the resolution. Subcreation can make money where the brand owner gains from it, and those owners already have the licenses to do it. Where they don't gain, no platform can make them want to.

## For anyone asking the same question

If you're asking what I was asking, here is what I'd tell you.

- **If you own a world and want everyone to build on it:** use Creative Commons. You don't need anyone's platform.
- **If you own a world and want value to come back:** create your own RPG and publish a third-party license like Lancer's, or use ORC. Keep your brand attached; it's the channel the value travels through.
- **If you're a fan who wants to earn:** look for worlds whose owners offer a license, which today mostly means tabletop games. Elsewhere, build your own brand, or give your work freely, as fans always have.
- **If you want to build something for this:** never make anyone register what they make. And look at what IP holders already do before you design anything. The market had the answer the whole time.
